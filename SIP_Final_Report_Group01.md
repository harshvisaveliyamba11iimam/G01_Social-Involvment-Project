## 1. Executive Summary

Amritsar is known across the world for the Golden Temple, its history, and its food. Yet, in the quiet back alleys of the city and nearby historic towns like Jandyala Guru, an invaluable cultural heritage survives in the hands of traditional craftspeople. These artisans shape brass and copper utensils, forge sacred Kirpans, mold clay tandoors by hand, stretch leather over classical tablas, and carve wooden chess knights with chisels.

Despite decades of master craftsmanship, these artisans face acute economic vulnerability. Machine-made factory alternatives (such as cheap stainless steel and automated tools) flood markets. Middlemen capture retail markups. Young family members leave for other careers, putting centuries-old craft lineages on the brink of extinction.

As part of the Social Involvement Project (SIP) at IIM Amritsar, Group 01 carried out an on-ground documentation and visibility project in partnership with Punjabi Samvad NGO. Rather than writing abstract proposals, our primary objective was tangible and practical: go directly to the workshops, talk to the artisans, record their true stories and economic realities, and give them a permanent, searchable public platform that did not exist before.

The core outcomes of this project are fully functional and verifiable today: an interactive live website ([art-heritage-of-amritsar.onrender.com](https://art-heritage-of-amritsar.onrender.com)), an active Instagram documentary handle ([@heritageamritsar2026](https://www.instagram.com/heritageamritsar2026?srtk=eXg5Z2JiZmloNjg2)), and a central Google Drive archive containing raw interviews, audio recordings, video clips, and transcripts.

## 2. Problem Statement & Objectives

### 2.1 The Core Problem
Traditional artisans do the hardest, most skilled manual labor, but earn the smallest share of the final price. When consumers buy handicraft products in retail showrooms or tourist markets, they pay high prices, yet the maker who sat by a wood fire or grinding wheel often takes home barely a subsistence wage. 

Furthermore, because artisans are completely absent from the internet, consumers cannot contact them directly, learn the labor behind the product, or appreciate the difference between authentic handmade craft and factory replicas.

### 2.2 Primary Objectives
1. **Direct Field Documentation:** Visit workshops across Amritsar city and the Thathera craft cluster in Jandyala Guru to capture firsthand interviews, working methods, and pricing realities.
2. **Preserving Living History:** Record oral histories of elder artisans before their knowledge disappears, including Partition memories and multi-generational lineages.
3. **Public Visibility Platform:** Build and deploy a fast, elegant, mobile-friendly website that tells each artisan's story by name with high-resolution photography, quotes, and craft details.
4. **Social Media Outreach:** Run an Instagram page to take these stories to young urban audiences and cultural tourists across India and abroad.
5. **Community Partnership:** Collaborate with an active local grassroots NGO (Punjabi Samvad) to ensure ethical engagement and continuity.

## 3. Partnership with Punjabi Samvad NGO

To ground our work in community action, our group partnered with **Punjabi Samvad**, a registered NGO based in Amritsar (293 Green Avenue, Amritsar; [punjabisamvad.com](https://punjabisamvad.com/)), led by President Ms. Jyoti Bawa.

Punjabi Samvad works with a simple, three-word philosophy: **Dialogue, Dignity, Change**. This matched our project purpose directly:
* **Dialogue:** Instead of speaking for artisans from a distance, we engaged in direct dialogue with them in their own workshops, letting them tell their own stories in their own words.
* **Dignity:** We documented artisans with deep respect, highlighting their skill, names, locations, and generational dignity rather than portraying them merely as subjects of charity.
* **Change:** We leveraged modern digital tools to bridge the gap between traditional craft and modern consumers, promoting sustainable awareness.

Punjabi Samvad is prominently featured on our live website and project channels. The NGO has officially acknowledged and certified the completion of this project.

## 4. Fieldwork & Methodology

Our fieldwork took place across multiple craft clusters in Amritsar and Jandyala Guru. We followed a structured, ethical methodology:
1. **Preparation & Outreach:** We identified active artisan clusters through local inquiries, craft contacts, and ground exploration in areas like Jandyala Guru, Manna Singh Chowk, and local roadside workshops.
2. **Consent & In-Person Dialogue:** We explained the educational and non-commercial purpose of our project to each artisan before recording. Interviews were conducted in Hindi and Punjabi.
3. **Standard Inquiry Framework:** Each interview covered how they learned the craft, how long a piece takes to make, raw material sourcing, daily production, family history, economic returns, competition from machines, and their message to the world.
4. **Multi-Media Capture:** We captured high-resolution photographs of artisans and their workspaces, recorded video demonstrations of techniques, and gathered audio transcripts.
5. **Translation & Archiving:** Audio recordings were translated and transcribed into written accounts, and all source assets were organized on a shared Google Drive for public verification.

## 5. The Artisans & Their Stories

Our fieldwork documented five distinct craft traditions across six artisans, each representing a unique chapter of Amritsar’s living heritage.

### 5.1 Harimandar ji — The Thathera (Brass & Copper Smith)
* **Location & Legacy:** Jandyala Guru, Amritsar district. The Thathera craft colony dates back nearly 200 years to the era of Maharaja Ranjit Singh and was inscribed on the UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2014.
* **The Craft & Process:** Copper and brass sheets from Jagadari (Haryana) are heated over open wood fires and hammered by hand. Strike after strike, flat metal becomes *kadais*, *paraats*, *lotas*, and glasses. One artisan produces only 3 to 4 glasses per day, entirely by hand.
* **Artisan Quote:** *“There is nothing in the machine. It is handmade. It is a proper handmade.”*
* **Core Challenge & Hope:** Stainless steel cookware decimated demand, leaving barely 5% of the original artisan colony active. However, collaborative revival models (such as Zishta) have brought steady year-round income, proving that when fair value and modern markets meet authentic craft, young artisans return.

### 5.2 Husan ji, Kuldip ji & Sahib Singh ji — Sacred Steel (Kirpan Forgers)
* **Location & Legacy:** Kirpan Workshop, Amritsar. Over 70 years of metal forging starting around 1950.
* **The Craft & Process:** Crafting a sacred Kirpan is a collaborative process passing through 8 to 10 pairs of hands across 10 to 12 distinct steps: cutting, washing oxide, hammering, grinding, polishing, fitting hilt handles, and tailoring decorative fabric and leather sheaths.
* **Pricing Spectrum:** Simple daily-wear Kirpans start at ₹250, while gold/silver embellished ceremonial blades for weddings and gurudwaras go up to ₹1,00,000–₹2,00,000. Products are exported worldwide, including to the USA and Canada.
* **Core Challenge:** Husan ji expressed deep concern about generational loss: *“The new generation is getting dark... after this, there may only be machinery.”* A machine-stamped blade lacks the spiritual dedication and manual mastery of hand-forged steel.

### 5.3 Manga ji — Fire & Clay (The Tandoor Maker)
* **Location & Heritage:** Roadside workshop under a banyan tree, Amritsar. A three-generation family craft brought from Pakistan during the 1947 Partition.
* **The Craft & Process:** Manga ji shapes large clay ovens by hand using pure earth and water—no wheel, no mechanical molds. He coils wet clay upward and kneads it with his palms to withstand high coal-fired cooking temperatures.
* **Artisan Quote:** *“My grandmother used to make it, my mother used to make it, and I make it. We have been making tandoors from Pakistan to India.”*
* **Economic Reality:** Manga ji sells tandoors for as little as ₹300 each. Business peaks in winter when homes and roadside dhabas cook over coal. Despite hardship, his only request was simple: he welcomed people to call him, visit his stand, and listen to his story.

### 5.4 Siraj Ahmed ji — Rhythm in Leather (The Tabla Maker)
* **Location & Experience:** Manna Singh Chowk, Amritsar. Over 20 years of craftsmanship, originating from Uttar Pradesh, practicing in Amritsar for 15+ years.
* **The Craft & Process:** Siraj ji handcrafts professional tabla pairs for renowned classical ragis and Punjabi University music scholars. A single pair takes 10 to 12 days. Drum heads and straps are hand-treated goatskin, while shells are shaped from copper and brass.
* **Artisan Quote:** *“I work as an artist. I don’t make it so quickly... There is a difference between making a tabla and truly making a tabla.”*
* **Philosophy & Coexistence:** As a Muslim artisan crafting instruments for sacred Sikh hymns and classical music, Siraj ji reflects the composite culture of Amritsar. He noted that while cheap factory tablas exist, genuine musicians always seek handmade sound quality.

### 5.5 Surinder Pal Singh ji — The Knight’s Move (Chess Carver)
* **Location & Training:** Amritsar. Apprenticed under a master carver since the 8th grade.
* **The Craft & Process:** Specializes in hand-carving wooden chess pieces, particularly the Knight (horse), the most intricate piece on the board. The artisan must sculpt muscle, mane, and nostrils out of a solid block of wood using fine chisels.
* **Artisan Philosophy:** *“The best game and the best exercise of the mind is chess. People should know how hard it is to make these pieces.”* Surinder ji creates both tournament sets and custom gold/silver finished collector pieces.
* **Generational Outlook:** Surinder ji shared a progressive view: while his children may learn the craft, he believes the next generation must have the freedom to choose their own career path without pressure.

## 7. Project Limitations & Field Adaptations

During execution, our on-ground experience led to an important adaptation from our original proposal:

### 7.1 Ground Discovery Regarding Phulkari Craft
In our initial project proposal, we planned to cover Phulkari hand-embroidery alongside other crafts. However, during our field investigation in Amritsar’s textile markets (including Katra Jaimal Singh and Hall Bazaar) and upon visiting the official Punjab Government Phulkari Emporium, we discovered an important ground reality: **Phulkari is not manufactured or embroidered in Amritsar.**

Local merchants and emporium officials clarified that almost all authentic hand-embroidered Phulkari is produced in Patiala and surrounding rural villages in southern Punjab. The shops in Amritsar only trade finished Phulkari dupattas and suits, many of which are now machine-stitched powerloom copies from textile mills. 

Because our project mission was to highlight actual grassroots craftspeople rather than wholesale middlemen, we decided not to profile retail showroom owners as artisans.

### 7.2 Scope Adjustment
We refocused our efforts entirely on genuine makers based in Amritsar and Jandyala Guru who physically craft each piece by hand. This pivot enriched our study with five deeply authentic, diverse craft forms: Thathera metalwork, sacred Kirpan forging, earthen tandoors, classical tablas, and hand-carved chess pieces.

## 8. Practical Recommendations

Based on our direct conversations with artisans, we recommend four practical interventions:
1. **QR-Code Heritage Labels:** Showrooms and local tourism departments should place simple QR codes on authentic handcrafted goods. When scanned by a tourist, the code can show the artisan's picture, workshop location, and making process. This immediately builds willingness to pay fair prices.
2. **Direct Institutional Sourcing:** Hotels, restaurants, and educational institutions in Punjab should source cooking vessels, tandoors, and musical instruments directly from artisan colonies like Jandyala Guru, eliminating middleman cuts.
3. **Artisan Experience Trails:** Tourism boards should include craft clusters (such as the Jandyala Guru Thathera lanes and Manna Singh Chowk) in official heritage walking tours of Amritsar, enabling tourists to watch crafts live and buy directly.
4. **Digital Enablement by Student Groups:** Student projects at institutions like IIM Amritsar should continue building digital assets (websites, social media, cataloging) for informal craft clusters, creating long-term visibility without charging fees.

## 9. Conclusion

The Social Involvement Project provided our team with an invaluable opportunity to engage deeply with the living culture of Amritsar. We learned that the true heritage of a city does not reside solely in its monuments; it lives in the daily labor, patience, and dignity of its craftspeople.

By combining fieldwork, media production, and NGO collaboration, Group 01 has built a permanent public record that honors the hands that built Amritsar.
